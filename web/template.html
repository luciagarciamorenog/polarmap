The day's debates

Nobody gives the model a list of topics. It reads the day's headlines, finds what the press is arguing about and defines the two opposing sides of each debate. Pick a debate to see where each outlet stands.

Two daily scores per outlet, computed from its headlines in the day's debates. One-sidedness: how far its coverage sits from the centre, whichever side (0 = balanced, 1 = fully one side). Hostility: share of its headlines that attack or belittle the other side.

Methodology

A Python pipeline plus three calls to the Claude API. Every number on this page comes from one daily run; nothing is computed in your browser except the charts.

  1. CollectEvery Spanish news article logged by the open GDELT project in 24 hours. The real headline and lead are read from each page. Publishers that reserve text-and-data-mining rights are excluded.
  2. Find the debatesClaude Sonnet 4.6 reads the day's headlines and proposes the main debates, each defined by two opposing positions. No predefined topics.
  3. AssignClaude Haiku 4.5 assigns every headline to one debate, or to none. Most news is not a fight.
  4. ScoreClaude Sonnet 4.6 scores each headline's stance (−1 to +1 between the two sides) and hostility, following a written codebook and reasoning before it scores.
  5. MeasurePython computes the polarization indices and each outlet's position with 90% bootstrap intervals and permutation tests. Debates with too few headlines or outlets are flagged.

What is measured

  • Ideological polarization: do outlets cluster into separate camps? Esteban-Ray index, which rewards separated groups, not mere disagreement. Spread-out opinions are diversity, not polarization.
  • Affective polarization: hostility concentrated at the extremes and aimed at the other side. Generic negativity does not count.
  • Stance, not sentiment: an earthquake is negative news but not divisive. The model scores which side a headline takes.
  • Labels such as "high conflict" use provisional thresholds (0.15 and 0.30).

Limitations

  • It measures published media, not public opinion.
  • Coverage per outlet depends on what GDELT collects.
  • The two sides of each debate are written by the model each day. They are stored and published for audit.
  • No outlet is labelled left or right. Its position comes only from what it publishes.
  • Spain only, for now.

What's next

  • A run every day, so outlets can be tracked over time.
  • Other European countries and languages, for a comparable measure across Europe.
  • Benchmark models, including open-weight ones, on accuracy and cost for the scoring step.
  • A Q&A agent (RAG) on top of this page, grounded in PolarMap's data and in conflict research such as Amanda Ripley's High Conflict, to help people understand where polarization comes from and how to navigate it.
